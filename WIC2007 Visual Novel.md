# WIC2007 Visual Novel: CyberTok - Think Before You Click

## Core Question

How can a cybersecurity awareness initiative effectively educate teenagers (13-18 years old) about online predators, help them identify grooming behavior, and equip them with practical safety steps?

This visual novel answers that question by turning cybersecurity lessons into interactive choices with immediate consequences.

## Target Audience

- Primary: Teenagers aged 13-18
- Secondary: Parents, teachers, and school counselors for guided discussion

## Learning Goals

By the end of the story, players should be able to:

1. Identify common online threat tactics used against teens.
2. Recognize grooming patterns early.
3. Apply practical cybersecurity and personal safety responses.
4. Know when and how to report incidents.
5. Understand that asking trusted adults for help is a strength, not a weakness.

## Instructional Design for Awareness Initiative

### 1. Scenario-Based Learning

- Players make realistic social media choices.
- Every risky decision triggers believable consequences.
- Every safe decision reinforces a practical defense habit.

### 2. Emotional Realism + Safety Debrief

- Story moments are emotionally relatable to teens.
- Critical choices are followed by short debriefs explaining why a tactic is dangerous and what to do next.

### 3. Actionable Cyber Hygiene

- Use privacy controls intentionally.
- Limit personal information sharing.
- Verify identity claims.
- Preserve evidence (screenshots, usernames, timestamps).
- Block, report, and escalate to trusted adults.

### 4. Age-Appropriate Language

- Uses teen voice and local Malaysian context.
- Avoids heavy lecture style while keeping safety guidance clear.

## Story Setup

You are a 14-year-old teen (gender neutral) who just got a first laptop and opens a CyberTok account while waiting for your friend, FaizTheGoat.

You choose:

- Username
- Public or private account
- First post
- How to respond to suspicious interactions

Your choices lead to 1 good ending or 4 bad endings.

[CHOICE 3: First Post]

A) OOTD in school uniform with school name cues.

B) Laptop setup photo with less personal identity data.

Cybersecurity note:
- Oversharing location, school identity, schedules, and routines increases targeting risk.

## Route 1: Grooming (AbangMer.11)

[BGM: Soft acoustic]

A friendly gamer messages you, offers free help, and starts building trust.

### Grooming Pattern Progression

1. Friendly entry: Shared hobby and compliments.
2. Personal probing: Asks age, family stress, emotional vulnerabilities.
3. Dependency building: "Only I understand you."
4. Isolation attempt: Undermines friends and family.
5. Secrecy plus platform migration: "Move to Telegram, keep it secret."
6. Incentive hook: Gifts, game currency, favors.

### Critical Choice

A) Accept secrecy and migrate to private chat.

B) Refuse secrecy, block, and report.

### Cybersecurity Skills Taught

- Red flags: secrecy requests, fast emotional closeness, isolation from trusted people, gift-based manipulation.
- Response: do not move to hidden channels, preserve evidence, block/report, tell parent/guardian/school counselor.

---

## Route 2: Catfish and Sextortion (U_look_gut)

[BGM: Slow romance tone]

An attractive account showers you with attention and quickly pushes intimacy.

### Attack Pattern

1. Love-bombing and validation.
2. Move to disappearing-message platform.
3. Pressure for revealing image.
4. Capture content externally.
5. Monetary extortion and threats.

### Critical Choice

A) Pay to stop the threat.

B) Do not pay. Screenshot evidence, block/report, and seek adult help immediately.

### Cybersecurity Skills Taught

- Red flags: rushed intimacy, guilt tactics, requests for explicit content, claims that disappearing chats are safe.
- Response: never send intimate content under pressure, never pay blackmail, document evidence, escalate immediately.

---

## Route 3: Scam and Financial Social Engineering (The_Si_Kamar)

[BGM: Fast and uneasy]

A fake business account offers easy income and pushes urgency.

### Attack Pattern

1. Opportunity bait: easy money for students.
2. Fake social proof: edited bank screenshots.
3. Urgency: limited slots, "act now."
4. Authority simulation: fake onboarding flow.
5. Credential theft: bank card details plus TAC code request.

### Critical Choice

A) Share TAC and card details.

B) Reject, report, and inform parent immediately.

### Cybersecurity Skills Taught

- Red flags: too-good-to-be-true earnings, urgency pressure, requests for banking secrets.
- Response: never share TAC/OTP/PIN/card details, verify opportunities through trusted adults, report scam accounts.

---

## Route 4: Cyberbullying and Harassment Escalation (BawangGirlzzz)

[BGM: Low tense drones]

A bully attacks your post, edits your content into memes, and mobilizes pile-ons.

### Attack Pattern

1. Public insult and provocation.
2. Content misuse and humiliation meme creation.
3. Crowd amplification and DM harassment.
4. Emotional trigger to force reactive posting.

### Critical Choice

A) Rage-reply publicly.

B) Disengage, collect evidence, block/mute, and seek support.

### Cybersecurity Skills Taught

- Red flags: bait comments, identity targeting, coordinated harassment.
- Response: do not feed rage-bait, document evidence, adjust privacy, involve school and guardian support.

---

## Good Ending: Secure Behavior Wins

You identify red flags across all routes and take protective action:

- Limit exposure through privacy and mindful posting.
- Refuse secrecy and manipulation.
- Never share financial or intimate content under pressure.
- Preserve evidence and report abuse.
- Involve trusted adults early.

Faiz confirms each incident as a known cyber threat pattern and reinforces practical habits.

Learning outcome message:

Digital safety is not one setting. It is a set of repeatable decisions.

---

## Bad Endings: Why Unsafe Choices Fail

Each bad ending demonstrates one cybersecurity principle:

1. Grooming ending: secrecy plus isolation increases real-world danger.
2. Sextortion ending: payment encourages repeated extortion.
3. Scam ending: sharing TAC enables immediate financial theft.
4. Cyberbully ending: public retaliation fuels harassment spread.

Debrief rule:
- Every bad ending is followed by a "What to do instead" recap slide.

## Practical Safety Toolkit (Shown In-Game)

### Stop-Check-Share Rule

1. Stop: Pause before replying to pressure messages.
2. Check: Ask, "Is this trying to rush, flatter, isolate, or scare me?"
3. Share: Tell a trusted adult before taking risky action.

### Teen Cyber Defense Checklist

- Keep accounts private where possible.
- Avoid posting school, routine, address, or live location details.
- Do not move to secret chats with strangers.
- Never share TAC/OTP/PIN/passwords.
- Never send intimate images when pressured.
- Screenshot, block, report, and escalate.

### Trusted Help Pathway (Malaysia Context)

- Parent or guardian
- School counselor or discipline teacher
- Cyber999 and official reporting channels
- Platform in-app abuse reporting

## Facilitator Add-On (School or Campaign Use)

To make the initiative effective beyond gameplay:

1. Pre-session poll to assess current teen cyber risk awareness.
2. Guided play in small groups for each critical choice.
3. Post-route debrief mapping threat tactic to defense action.
4. Parent handout with home discussion prompts and reporting resources.
5. Follow-up quiz after 1-2 weeks to test behavior retention.

Success indicators:

- Improved red-flag recognition scores
- Higher confidence in reporting
- Better evidence preservation habits
- Increased willingness to involve trusted adults

## Final Message to Players

You are not weak for asking for help.

Predators and scammers rely on silence, urgency, and shame.

Your strongest cybersecurity tools are:

- Awareness
- Boundaries
- Evidence
- Reporting
- Trusted support
